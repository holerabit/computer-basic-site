What Is a Power Supply and Why Does It Matter?

The Unsung Hero of Every PC

The power supply unit is the most overlooked component in any PC build — yet it is the one component that powers everything else. The PSU takes alternating current (AC) from your wall outlet, typically at 110V or 220V depending on your country, and converts it into the direct current (DC) voltages that computer components require. Modern PCs use three main voltage rails: 12V for the CPU, GPU, and drives; 5V for USB and some older components; and 3.3V for RAM and certain motherboard circuits.

A poor quality or underpowered PSU is one of the most common causes of system instability, random shutdowns, and in worst cases permanent hardware damage. Unlike a CPU or GPU, a failing PSU can take other components with it. This is why experienced builders consistently advise spending more on a quality power supply rather than cutting costs there.

PSU capacity is measured in watts. A basic office PC might need 300–400W. A gaming PC with a mid-range GPU typically needs 550–650W. A high-end system with an RTX 5090 or RX 9070 XT can require 850W or more. The general rule is to choose a PSU with about 20–30% headroom above your system's calculated power draw — this improves efficiency and extends the unit's lifespan.

Efficiency ratings are certified by the 80 PLUS program. A Bronze rated PSU is at least 82% efficient under typical load, meaning 18% of drawn power is lost as heat. Gold is 87–90% efficient, Platinum is 90–92%, and Titanium reaches 94% or higher. Higher efficiency means lower electricity bills and less heat inside the case. For most builds, Gold certification is the sweet spot between cost and efficiency.

Modular PSUs are another important distinction. A fully modular PSU lets you connect only the cables you need, reducing clutter and improving airflow. Semi-modular PSUs have fixed main cables but detachable peripheral cables. Non-modular PSUs have all cables permanently attached — cheaper but messy. For any build where cable management matters, modular is worth the small price premium.

Who Makes Power Supplies?

The PSU market has many brands but only a handful of actual manufacturers who produce the internal components. Seasonic, based in Taiwan, is widely considered the gold standard of PSU manufacturing. They manufacture units for their own brand and also produce PSUs sold under ASUS, Corsair, and other labels. A Seasonic-made PSU is found inside many premium units regardless of the brand name on the box.

Corsair is the most recognizable consumer PSU brand globally. Their RMx and HXi series are among the most popular enthusiast choices, offering full modularity, long warranty periods (up to 10 years), and consistent quality. Corsair does not manufacture internally — they partner with CWT and Seasonic for production.

be quiet! is a German brand known for extremely quiet operation. Their Straight Power and Dark Power series are favorites among builders who prioritize silence. They manufacture through their own ODM partners and maintain strict quality standards. be quiet! PSUs are particularly popular in Europe.

EVGA was a major PSU brand until they exited the GPU and PSU market in 2022. Their SuperNOVA series was highly regarded. Fractal Design, primarily known for cases, also produces quality PSUs through their Ion series. Super Flower is a Taiwanese manufacturer whose units are sold under multiple brand names and are known for excellent build quality at competitive prices

What PSU Should You Buy?

The honest answer depends entirely on your build. For a mid-range gaming PC with an RTX 4070 or RX 7800 XT class GPU, a 650–750W Gold rated fully modular unit from Seasonic, Corsair, or be quiet! is the right choice. Do not buy a no-name PSU to save $20 — it is the only component that can destroy everything else if it fails.

For high-end builds with an RTX 5090 or dual-GPU workstation setups, 1000W or above with Platinum or Titanium efficiency is worth the investment. The electricity savings over several years partially offset the higher upfront cost.

Always check the warranty length — a 10-year warranty signals that the manufacturer is confident in their product's longevity. Seasonic's 12-year warranty on their Prime series is the longest in the industry and a strong indicator of build quality.
