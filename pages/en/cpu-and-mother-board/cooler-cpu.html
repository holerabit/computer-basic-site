CPU Coolers: Then and Now

Early CPU Cooling: When a Small Fan Was Enough

The first consumer CPUs did not need dedicated coolers at all. The Intel 486 (1989) ran cool enough that many configurations used only a heatsink — a block of aluminum fins that passively dissipated heat into the air. As clock speeds climbed through the 1990s, a small fan attached to the heatsink became necessary. These early cooler designs were simple: a square aluminum heatsink with a 60mm fan clipped directly on top.

The Pentium era of the mid-1990s standardized the heatsink-fan (HSF) design that still forms the basis of air cooling today. Intel and AMD began including stock coolers with their retail CPU boxes — basic but functional units designed to keep the processor within safe temperatures under typical workloads. These stock coolers were universally loud and often inadequate for sustained loads.

The late 1990s and early 2000s saw the first enthusiast aftermarket coolers. Companies like Thermalright and Zalman introduced larger heatsinks with more surface area and quieter fans. Zalman's flower-shaped coolers from the early 2000s became iconic — radially arranged fins that drew air from all directions were unusual but effective. By 2005, the tower cooler design emerged: a large heatsink with heatpipes running vertically, oriented to work with the case's front-to-back airflow. This design dominates air cooling to this day.

Liquid cooling first appeared in enthusiast builds in the early 2000s as custom loops: pumps, reservoirs, waterblocks, and radiators assembled by hand. It was expensive, complex, and prone to leaks — but dramatically more effective than any air cooler for overclocked CPUs. The first all-in-one (AIO) liquid coolers appeared around 2007–2010, making liquid cooling accessible without the complexity of a custom loop.

Modern CPU Cooling: Air, AIO, and Custom Loops

Modern CPU cooling falls into three categories. High-end air coolers represent the pinnacle of passive-assisted cooling. Units like the Noctua NH-D15 and be quiet! Dark Rock Pro 5 use multiple heatpipes, large dual-tower heatsinks, and slow, large-diameter fans to move enormous amounts of heat silently. A good high-end air cooler can handle any consumer CPU including the 170W AMD Ryzen 9 9950X and rivals entry-level AIO liquid coolers in performance.

All-in-one (AIO) liquid coolers are the most popular choice for mid-to-high-end builds. An AIO consists of a CPU waterblock connected via tubes to a radiator mounted in the case, with fans attached to the radiator. The coolant circulates in a sealed loop requiring no maintenance. AIO radiators come in 120mm, 240mm, 280mm, and 360mm sizes — larger radiators dissipate more heat. A 360mm AIO can handle even the most power-hungry CPUs like the Intel Core Ultra 9 285K under heavy overclocking.

Custom water cooling loops remain the domain of enthusiasts willing to invest significant time and money. A custom loop allows cooling multiple components (CPU, GPU, motherboard VRMs) with a single system. The components — pump, reservoir, waterblocks, radiators, fittings, and tubing — are assembled by the builder. Performance is superior to any AIO but the cost can exceed $500 for a complete loop, and maintenance including coolant replacement is required annually.

Thermal paste is a critical but often overlooked part of CPU cooling. It fills microscopic gaps between the CPU's heat spreader and the cooler's base plate, dramatically improving heat transfer. Most coolers ship with pre-applied paste, but aftermarket options like Thermal Grizzly Kryonaut or Noctua NT-H2 can lower temperatures by 3–8°C compared to generic pastes.

Major CPU Cooler Manufacturers

Noctua — The Performance Standard

Noctua is an Austrian company founded in 2005 as a joint venture between Rascom and Kolink International. They are universally regarded as producing the best air coolers and fans available. Their NH-D15 dual-tower cooler has held the top position in air cooling benchmarks for over a decade with only minor revisions. Noctua's fans are known for their exceptional balance of airflow, static pressure, and noise — their NF-A12x25 fan is considered the reference standard against which all others are measured.

Noctua's distinctive brown and beige color scheme has become one of the most recognized aesthetics in PC building — loved by some for its performance association, mocked by others for its appearance. In 2020, Noctua released chromax black versions of their most popular products for builders who want Noctua performance without the signature colors. Their products carry a 6-year warranty and come with SecuFirm mounting systems that make installation easier than most competitors.

be quiet! — Silence as a Priority

be quiet! is a German brand under the parent company Listan GmbH, founded in 2001. As their name suggests, silence is their primary design goal. Their Dark Rock Pro 5 and Shadow Rock 3 air coolers combine high performance with extremely low noise output. be quiet! achieves this through optimized fan blade profiles that minimize turbulence and vibration-dampening mounts that prevent noise from transferring to the case.

Beyond air coolers, be quiet! produces the Silent Loop 2 AIO series and the Pure Loop 2 for more budget-conscious builds. Their coolers are particularly popular in Europe where noise regulations in office environments and living spaces make quiet operation more important. be quiet! products are widely available across Europe and increasingly popular in North America.

Cooler Master — The Accessible Option

Cooler Master, founded in Taiwan in 1992, is one of the oldest and most prolific cooling brands. Their Hyper 212 series is one of the best-selling CPU coolers in history — an affordable single-tower cooler that has offered excellent value for over 15 years. Cooler Master occupies the mid-range and budget segments effectively, making quality cooling accessible to builders on tight budgets.

Their MasterLiquid AIO series covers 240mm through 360mm radiator sizes at competitive prices. Cooler Master also produces the MasterAir series of air coolers ranging from budget to high-end. Their products lack the premium feel of Noctua or be quiet! but consistently deliver good performance per dollar.

ARCTIC — Value Engineering

ARCTIC is a Swiss company that has built a strong reputation for delivering exceptional performance at low prices. Their Freezer 34 and Freezer 36 air coolers regularly outperform coolers costing twice as much in independent benchmarks. ARCTIC achieves this by focusing purely on thermal performance rather than RGB lighting or premium aesthetics.

Their Liquid Freezer II AIO series disrupted the market by offering 240mm and 360mm AIOs at prices well below established competitors while matching or exceeding their thermal performance. The Liquid Freezer II includes a small VRM fan on the pump head that cools motherboard components around the CPU socket — a thoughtful feature not found on most competitors. ARCTIC products have become the go-to recommendation for budget-conscious builders who refuse to compromise on cooling performance.

What Should You Buy?

For most builders, the choice comes down to budget and goals. If you want the simplest, most reliable solution and your case has clearance for a large tower, a Noctua NH-D15 or Thermalright Peerless Assassin 120 will handle any consumer CPU without ever needing maintenance. The Thermalright in particular offers performance that rivals coolers three times its price.

If you want lower CPU temperatures for overclocking or simply prefer the aesthetics of liquid cooling, a 240mm or 360mm AIO is the right choice. The ARCTIC Liquid Freezer II is the value champion — it performs at the level of coolers costing twice as much. For premium builds, the Corsair H150i or NZXT Kraken 360 offer polished software integration and reliable performance.

Custom loops are only worth considering if you plan to cool both your CPU and GPU, want to push extreme overclocks, or simply enjoy the build process. The performance advantage over a good AIO is real but modest for CPU-only cooling. The maintenance requirement and higher cost make them impractical for most users.

One final note: always check that your chosen cooler is compatible with your CPU socket before buying. Intel's socket has changed from LGA1700 (12th–14th gen) to LGA1851 (Core Ultra 200 series). AMD's AM5 socket has remained consistent since 2022 and is committed through at least 2027, meaning AM5 coolers will remain compatible for several CPU generations.
