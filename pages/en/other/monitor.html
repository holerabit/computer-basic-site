Old Monitors: From CRT to LCD

The CRT Era

The cathode ray tube monitor dominated computing from the 1970s through the early 2000s. CRT displays work by firing electron beams at a phosphor-coated screen — the beam scans across the screen thousands of times per second, illuminating pixels to form an image. The technology was inherited from television sets and adapted for computer use.

Early computer monitors were monochrome — green or amber text on a black background. Color CRTs became standard through the 1980s. IBM's introduction of the VGA standard in 1987 established 640x480 resolution as the baseline, with 256 colors. Through the 1990s, resolutions climbed to 800x600 and then 1024x768 as the standard for everyday computing.

CRTs had genuine strengths that LCD monitors initially could not match: perfect black levels, near-zero input lag, and the ability to display any resolution natively without scaling artifacts. Professional artists and gaming enthusiasts appreciated these qualities. However, CRTs were heavy — a 19-inch CRT weighed 20–25 kg — consumed significant power, and emitted noticeable electromagnetic radiation. Their physical depth made them impractical for modern workspaces.

The LCD Transition

Liquid crystal display technology existed since the 1970s but was limited to calculators and watches. The first laptop LCD screens appeared in the mid-1980s — small, slow, and monochrome. Full-color active matrix LCD panels for desktop monitors became commercially viable in the late 1990s.

The transition happened rapidly between 2000 and 2005. LCD monitors were dramatically thinner and lighter than CRTs, consumed less power, and produced no electromagnetic emissions. Prices dropped quickly as manufacturing scaled up. By 2007, LCD monitors had largely replaced CRTs in consumer and professional markets. The last major CRT manufacturer stopped production around 2012.

Early LCDs had significant weaknesses compared to CRTs: slow response times caused motion blur in games and video, viewing angles were narrow with color shifting when viewed from the side, and contrast ratios were poor. These issues drove the development of multiple LCD panel technologies over the following decade.

Modern Monitors: Panels, Resolution, and Refresh Rates

Panel Technologies Today

Modern monitors use several distinct panel technologies, each with different strengths. IPS (In-Plane Switching) panels are the most popular for general use. They offer excellent color accuracy, wide viewing angles with minimal color shift, and good brightness. IPS panels are the standard choice for photo and video editing, office work, and general gaming. Their weakness is relatively poor contrast ratios — blacks appear as dark gray rather than true black.

VA (Vertical Alignment) panels offer the best contrast ratios of any LCD technology — typically 3000:1 to 6000:1 compared to IPS's 1000:1. This makes dark scenes in games and movies look significantly better. VA panels have improved viewing angles over older generations but still shift slightly when viewed from extreme angles. Response times have historically been slower than IPS, though modern VA panels have narrowed this gap considerably.

TN (Twisted Nematic) panels are the oldest LCD technology still in production. They offer the fastest response times — as low as 1ms — making them popular for competitive gaming where motion clarity is critical. However, TN panels have poor color accuracy and very limited viewing angles. They are rarely recommended outside of competitive esports use cases.

OLED (Organic Light Emitting Diode) panels represent the current premium standard. Unlike LCD panels which use a backlight, each OLED pixel emits its own light and can turn completely off. This produces perfect black levels and theoretically infinite contrast ratios. OLED monitors offer exceptional color accuracy, wide viewing angles, and extremely fast response times. The main concerns are burn-in risk from static content and higher cost compared to LCD alternatives.

Resolution and Refresh Rate

Resolution defines how many pixels the monitor displays. Full HD (1920x1080, also called 1080p) remains the most common resolution for gaming monitors due to lower GPU requirements. QHD (2560x1440, also called 1440p or 2K) has become the sweet spot for enthusiast gaming and professional work — sharper than 1080p without the extreme GPU demands of 4K. UHD (3840x2160, also called 4K) offers exceptional detail for content creation and media consumption but requires a powerful GPU for gaming.

Refresh rate measures how many times per second the monitor updates its image, expressed in Hz. A standard 60Hz monitor updates 60 times per second. Gaming monitors now commonly offer 144Hz, 165Hz, 240Hz, or even 360Hz refresh rates. Higher refresh rates make motion appear smoother and more responsive — the difference between 60Hz and 144Hz is immediately visible to most people. Beyond 240Hz, improvements become harder to perceive for most users.

Adaptive sync technology eliminates screen tearing by synchronizing the monitor's refresh rate with the GPU's output. NVIDIA's G-Sync and AMD's FreeSync both solve this problem — G-Sync requires NVIDIA hardware and a certified monitor, while FreeSync is an open standard that works with AMD and many NVIDIA GPUs.

Which Monitor Should You Buy?

For most users, an IPS monitor at 1440p and 144Hz or higher is the best all-around choice in 2025. It covers gaming, productivity, and content creation without significant compromise. Brands like LG, ASUS, and Samsung produce reliable IPS panels at various price points.

If you watch a lot of movies or play atmospheric games with dark scenes, a VA panel's contrast ratio makes a visible difference. The Samsung Odyssey G7 and similar curved VA monitors are worth considering for this use case.

OLED monitors have become more affordable and are now a legitimate choice for enthusiast buyers. The LG UltraGear OLED and ASUS ROG Swift OLED series offer an experience that LCD simply cannot match in terms of contrast and color. The burn-in risk is real but manageable — avoid leaving static content on screen for hours and use a screensaver.

TN panels are only worth considering for competitive esports players who prioritize the absolute fastest response times above all else and play in well-lit rooms where viewing angles do not matter.
